# Research note: mumbling — the gaps beyond the clear-speech evidence

**Date:** 25 September 2026 · **Why:** the owner named mumbling as the main problem. This note fills the gaps that `learner-profile-addendum.md` §3 left open.

**How the sources were checked.** The session's network policy blocked the main paper hosts (PubMed, PMC, ASHA journals, Crossref, ResearchGate, Semantic Scholar, Springer, ERIC). Every source was checked against its abstract text as returned by web search. No full text was opened. Any figure that did not appear in an abstract is marked **[Unverified]**. This note also verifies one lead that the addendum left unverified: the ASR-feedback study (Gutz et al. 2023).

## 1. Summary

- **Mumbling is a normal speaking style pushed too far.** It is not a disorder. In Lindblom's H&H (hyper- and hypo-articulation) theory, speakers save effort ("hypoarticulate") whenever they expect the listener to cope. Conversation shows heavy reduction: over 60% of words differ from their dictionary form (Johnson 2004). Reduced forms are recognised well only in context (Ernestus et al. 2002). **[Strong, descriptive]**
- **Only one peer-reviewed paper uses the word "mumbling" in its title.** It links mumbling to small jaw opening, which is larger in women and partly anatomical (Weirich et al. 2016). **[Weak–Moderate]**
- **Best cues, ranked:** (1) "clear / over-enunciate / big mouth", (2) "loud", (3) "slow". Clear and loud speech improved intelligibility in noise. Slow speech did not (Tjaden et al. 2014, 78 speakers). **[Moderate]**
- **Loudness spreads to articulation in healthy adults, but only within a session.** Loud speech brings larger lip and jaw movements and more pitch variation. Loudness *training* that spreads to articulation (LSVT LOUD) is proven only in Parkinson's disease. **[Moderate within a session; None found for training healthy adults]**
- **Precise articulation makes a voice sound louder at the same measured level** (Myers & Finnegan 2015). **[Weak–Moderate]**
- **Carry-over into daily speech:** no study found in healthy adults for clear or loud speech. The closest evidence is from nurses learning to speak *softer*. Summary feedback at the end of a block was retained better than constant feedback (Van Stan et al. 2017). **[None found / Moderate by analogy]**
- **A phone can measure change in level, not absolute level,** if the distance is fixed and auto-gain is off. Articulation rate, pauses, pitch range and final fade are feasible. Vowel-space measures are fragile. **[Moderate / Mixed]**

## 2. What mumbling is

| Feature | Evidence | Strength | Can a phone measure it? |
|---|---|---|---|
| A low-effort style (hypoarticulation) | H&H theory (Lindblom 1990). In an 88,000-word corpus, over 20% of words lost a sound and over 60% differed from the dictionary form (Johnson 2004). | Strong (descriptive) | Indirectly, through the rows below |
| Small jaw opening | Women opened the jaw more than men, in English and German data. A model suggests an anatomical cause (Weirich et al. 2016). Clear speech raised jaw movement more than loud or slow speech (Mefferd 2017, 20 typical talkers). | Moderate | Not directly. F1 of open vowels is a proxy; a front camera could track it. |
| Centralized vowels (formants drift toward a neutral "uh") | Clear speech enlarged vowel space the most in PD, MS and control speakers (Tjaden, Lam & Wilding 2013). Experimentally induced vowel contrast predicted intelligibility gains in healthy adults (Beach et al. 2024, preprint). | Moderate | Partly. Noise-sensitive (see section 5). |
| Low vocal level | Loud speech improved intelligibility in babble (Tjaden et al. 2014). | Moderate | Relative change only |
| Fading at phrase ends ("trailing off") | A drop in level toward the end of an utterance is normal in every language (Vaissière 1983). No study found that measures *excess* fading in healthy speakers or links it to intelligibility. | None found | Yes. It is a ratio inside one utterance. |
| Fast rate, run-together words | Speaking faster than habitual lowered intelligibility on average (46 speakers with PD or MS plus 32 healthy speakers). Some speakers did not decline (Kuo, Tjaden & Sussman 2014). | Moderate | Yes |
| Dropped word-final consonants | Common in casual speech (Johnson 2004; Brand & Ernestus 2021 for French). | Moderate (descriptive) | Hard. No validated detector found. |
| Narrow pitch range | Sentences resynthesized with a flat pitch were less intelligible (Laures & Weismer 1999; Watson & Schlauch 2008). Each used only two talkers. | Moderate | Yes, in semitones |
| Steep spectral tilt (little high-frequency energy) | A flatter tilt explained much of the intelligibility gain of speech produced in noise. Raising mean pitch did not (Lu & Cooke 2009). | Moderate | Relative only, same device |

Listener view: speech produced with a bite block ("poor articulation") was rated *less loud* and less "projected" than normal speech, with no difference in measured level. Over-articulation was rated louder (Myers & Finnegan 2015; 8 actors, 20 listeners). This matches the lay sense that a mumbler sounds "quiet".

## 3. Cues and training methods, ranked

1. **"Clear / over-enunciate / big mouth"** **[Moderate]**
   - Clear speech gave the largest vowel-space gains (Tjaden, Lam & Wilding 2013), improved intelligibility in babble (Tjaden, Sussman & Wilding 2014), and moved the jaw most (Mefferd 2017).
   - In 8 adolescents with Down syndrome, a "big mouth" cue raised intelligibility but a "strong voice" cue did not (Darling-White & McHugh 2025). **[Clinical]**
   - *Design:* make this the default cue.
2. **"Loud"** **[Moderate within a session, healthy adults]**
   - *Healthy adults:* loud speech enlarged lip movements and made them more consistent (Dromey & Ramig 1998, n=10). It amplified normal jaw and lip patterns (Schulman 1989, n=4). It enlarged sentence-level vowel space, but not the classic corner-vowel measures (Whitfield et al. 2018). It increased pitch variation, while slow speech reduced it (Tjaden & Wilding 2011).
   - *Loud vs slow:* loud speech maximized consonant distinctiveness and slow speech maximized vowel space (Tjaden & Wilding 2004; 15 MS, 12 PD, 15 controls).
   - *The wording of the cue matters:* 30 young adults all rose about 10 dB when told to hit a meter target, to speak "twice as loud", or to speak over noise. The meter target produced different lip and jaw movements from the other two cues (Huber & Chandrasekaran 2006).
   - *Clinical only:* after LSVT LOUD, vowel space and formant centralization ratio improved (Sapir et al. 2007). In a randomized controlled trial, LSVT LOUD beat articulation-focused therapy and no treatment for level at 1 and 7 months (Ramig et al. 2018, n=64). Only LSVT LOUD raised transcription intelligibility (Levy et al. 2020; the "31.5%" figure is **[Unverified]**). SPEAK OUT! raised level in an uncontrolled study (Behrman et al. 2020).
   - *Design:* use "fill the room" or listener framing, not a dB meter as the goal. Warn against straining the voice.
3. **"Open your jaw"** **[Weak–Moderate]**
   - Vowels spoken with an open jaw were identified better and judged clearer. The gain was not only due to extra level or length (J Voice 2016; 40 speakers aged 30s–80s, 40 listeners).
   - *Design:* use it as a concrete sub-cue of "clear".
4. **"Slow"** **[Moderate against it as a goal]**
   - Slow speech did not improve intelligibility (Tjaden et al. 2014), and it flattens pitch.
   - *Design:* use slowing only as a first step, then bring the speed back up.
5. **Noise or listener triggers (the Lombard effect: speaking louder and clearer in noise)** **[Moderate]**
   - Babble played through headphones produced larger changes than babble from loudspeakers. Feeding the speaker's own voice back into the headphones reduced this (Garnier, Henrich & Dubois 2010, French).
6. **Implicit training** **[Moderate, research-grade]**
   - Feedback that digitally made the speaker's vowels sound more centralized led speakers to exaggerate their vowels. The gains persisted after a delay, transferred to new words and predicted intelligibility (Beach et al. 2024, preprint).
   - *Design:* this needs real-time formant shifting. Future option only.

## 4. Carry-over into everyday speech

- **Healthy adults, clear or loud speech made habitual: None found.**
- **Feedback schedule [Moderate, healthy, opposite goal]:** 48 nurses with healthy voices wore a voice monitor to speak softer. Compliance rose sharply with feedback (d=4.5). Summary feedback lost less on retention days than 100% feedback (d≈1.1) (Van Stan et al. 2017).
- **Daily-life biofeedback [Weak, clinical]:** vibration alerts at low voice level helped only the participants with PD who had normal cognition; 9 of 16 completed (Körner Gustafsson et al. 2021). In PD, explicit instructions restored volume control where implicit cues failed (Ho et al. 1999).
- **Self-monitoring [Weak, children]:** self-monitoring outside the clinic sped generalization of corrected sounds in 13 children (Koegel et al. 1986).
- **ASR feedback [Moderate, within one session]:** 23 healthy adults given ASR accuracy scores produced speech similar to instructed clear speech, with more distinct vowels and a slower rate. ASR accuracy correlated with different features than human-judged intelligibility did (Gutz, Maffei & Green 2023).
- **Practice schedule [Weak]:** in 40 healthy adults, variable practice gave no advantage over constant practice (Kaipa 2016). Most motor-learning principles come from non-speech skills (Maas et al. 2008).

## 5. Measures for the app

| Measure | What it shows | Reliability on a phone | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| Speech level relative to the user's own baseline | Volume | Uncalibrated phones track *changes* in level well (linear fit R²>.98; agreement on differences ICC=.88), only with the same distance and settings (AJSLP 2025). Absolute dB is poor: 4 of 192 apps came within ±2 dB, all on iOS (Kardous & Shaw 2014). Level falls about 6 dB each time the distance doubles. Browsers turn on auto-gain, noise suppression and echo cancellation by default; turn them off (MDN). | Moderate (relative) |
| Phrase-final fade (last word vs the median of the utterance) | Trailing off | Measured inside one utterance, so distance and gain cancel out. Auto-gain can hide the fade, so it must be off. Not validated. | None found |
| Articulation rate (syllables per second, pauses excluded) and pauses | Pace | The syllable-nucleus method matched human syllable counts closely on Dutch (de Jong & Wempe 2009; exact r **[Unverified]**). Check it on French and English. | Moderate |
| Pitch range in semitones | Melody | Accurate on uncompressed phone audio. Loud speech widens it; slow speech narrows it. | Moderate |
| VSA / VAI / FCR (vowel space area, vowel articulation index, formant centralization ratio) | Vowel crispness | FCR is less sensitive to speaker differences (Sapir et al. 2010). VAI was most stable across 30 s–4 h (Caverlé & Vogel 2020). Corner-vowel measures missed loudness effects that sentence-level measures caught (Whitfield et al. 2018; Whitfield & Goberman 2014). Phone-call audio raised F1 by 29% (Byrne & Foulkes 2004), so record locally. Day-to-day reliability on a phone is unknown. | Mixed |
| Spectral tilt / 1–3 kHz energy | Brightness, projection | Depends on the microphone and distance. Compare only on the same device. | Mixed |
| ASR accuracy with added noise | Intelligibility proxy | Tracks clarity changes, but not the same features human listeners use (Gutz et al. 2023) | Mixed |

## 6. French-specific notes

- **Reduction is the norm in conversational French.** Most vowels are shorter and more centralized (Meunier & Espesser 2011). In 50,000 broadcast-news vowels, shorter vowels strayed further from their targets (Gendrot & Adda-Decker 2005). **[Moderate]** *Design:* ask for "full vowels", not "slow speech".
- **Word endings get dropped.** In casual Parisian French, 80.7% of 291 nouns ending in obstruent+liquid(+schwa) (*table, ministre*) lost at least one sound, and 15.5% lost the whole cluster (Brand & Ernestus 2021). Deletion rises in conversation (Boughton 2015). **[Moderate]** *Design:* drill *-ble, -tre, -dre, -pre* endings.
- **French hyperarticulation is visible.** In noise it affects lip opening and spreading more than rounding (Garnier et al. 2006, one speaker). Lip and tongue changes grew with face-to-face interaction (Garnier, Ménard & Alexandre 2018, 6 speakers). **[Weak–Moderate]**
- **French vowel metrics.** A study of 78 dysarthric speakers and 26 controls concluded that several complementary metrics are needed (Audibert & Fougeron 2012). A program that "misunderstands" the speaker elicited French clear speech (Robertson & Jongman 2024, conference abstract). **[Weak]**
- **"Mal articuler" in healthy French speakers: None found.** No French study on loudness training in healthy adults either.

## 7. What not to do

- **Cork, pen or pencil drills.** Only blogs support them. Speakers compensate for a bite block right away, but not completely when they cannot hear themselves (Murphy et al. 2026, n=55). Bite-block speech sounded less articulate and less loud (Myers & Finnegan 2015). A cork also blocks the jaw opening that mumblers under-use. **[None found for benefit; harm reasoned]**
- **Non-speech mouth exercises.** Insufficient evidence in adults with dysarthria (Kent 2015); no strong evidence in children (Lee & Gibbon 2015, Cochrane). **[Moderate against]**
- **"Just slow down."** No intelligibility gain, and it flattens pitch. **[Moderate]**
- **An absolute dB meter as the target.** Phones cannot measure it reliably, and a meter target changes movement patterns differently from other loudness cues. **[Moderate]**
- **Presenting LSVT or SPEAK OUT! results as proof for healthy adults.** **[Clinical only]**
- **Corner-vowel vowel space alone as a progress score.** **[Mixed]**
- **Tongue twisters.** **[None found]**

## 8. Design implications (ranked)

1. **Main cue: "Big and clear".** Open the jaw, give vowels their full shape, finish every ending. Loudness comes second, framed as reaching a listener. **[Moderate]**
2. **Paired habitual → clear takes, scored against the user's own baseline.** Profile: relative level, final fade, articulation rate, pauses, pitch range, VAI or sentence-level vowel space, spectral tilt. Lock the recording setup: a distance check, auto-gain, noise suppression and echo cancellation off, and the same device. **[Moderate]**
3. **Trailing-off detector** (last word vs the rest of the phrase). Cheap and robust to distance. Label it experimental. **[None found]**
4. **Word-endings module:** French obstruent+liquid clusters and English final stops. Label automatic detection as unvalidated. **[Moderate need; None found for detection]**
5. **Summary feedback after each block, faded over time, not a live meter.** **[Moderate, by analogy]**
6. **Listener and babble mode through earbuds** with a safe volume cap. Fade the noise out over sessions. **[Moderate]**
7. **ASR-in-noise as one feedback signal, never the only score.** **[Mixed]**
8. **Carry-over experiments:** a weekly 60–90 s free-speech check and optional reminders for real conversations, tracked as n-of-1 data. **[None found]**
9. **Future:** front-camera jaw and lip feedback, and vowel-perturbation training. **[Weak / research-grade]**
